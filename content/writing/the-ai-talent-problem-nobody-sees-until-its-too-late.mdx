## The workslop problem

AI makes producing things cheap...

The more I observe how organizations are adopting AI, the more I think we are asking the wrong question.

We keep asking:

"How much work can AI do?"

A more important question might be:

"What happens to the people who were supposed to learn by doing that work?"

That distinction matters.

Because organizations don't only produce products and services.

They also produce expertise.

And expertise doesn't appear magically.

It is accumulated through years of exposure to real problems, mistakes, repetition, feedback, responsibility, and increasingly difficult decisions.

This is where AI creates an interesting paradox.

AI can make an experienced employee dramatically more productive.

But the same AI can remove some of the work through which an inexperienced employee would have developed into that experienced employee.

That creates a delayed problem.

Today's productivity can become tomorrow's capability gap

Imagine a software organization.

A senior engineer uses AI to write, debug, refactor, test, and document code much faster.

From a short-term perspective, this looks excellent.

The organization gets more output from the same person.

Now imagine the organization decides:

"We don't need as many junior engineers anymore."

That decision can also look rational.

But where do future senior engineers come from?

They don't arrive at the organization as fully formed senior engineers.

They become senior engineers.

And becoming one requires exposure to the exact kinds of problems that automation may increasingly remove.

This is why I think the AI talent problem is deeper than layoffs or hiring numbers.

It is about the apprenticeship pipeline.

If we continuously remove the lower levels of the learning curve, we may eventually discover that we have optimized away the process that creates expertise.

The problem may not appear next quarter.

It may appear three, five, or ten years later.

By then, the organization may have very productive senior people and a surprisingly weak pipeline behind them.

That is a capability cliff.

Then there is the workslop problem

There is another failure mode.

AI makes producing things cheap.

That changes incentives.

If producing a report takes three hours, people naturally think about whether the report is necessary.

If AI allows ten reports to be produced in an hour, the organization may start rewarding volume instead.

And suddenly we have:

More documents.

More code.

More presentations.

More analysis.

More messages.

But not necessarily more value.

Someone still has to determine what is correct.

Someone still has to remove the nonsense.

Someone still has to understand the context.

Someone still has to make the final decision.

So the organization may not have eliminated work.

It may have changed the nature of the work from creation to verification and correction.

That can be useful.

But it can also become incredibly expensive if nobody measures the correction burden.

A system can look productive from the outside while quietly pushing more cognitive work onto the people responsible for quality.

The deeper issue is judgment

I think this is ultimately a judgment problem.

AI can generate.

AI can summarize.

AI can classify.

AI can recommend.

AI can write code.

But organizations still need people who can determine:

"Does this actually make sense?"

And that ability is not created by reading documentation.

It is developed through experience.

You need to encounter bad assumptions.

You need to make wrong decisions.

You need to debug systems that don't behave as expected.

You need to see how seemingly small decisions create large consequences.

You need to develop an internal model of how things actually work.

That is judgment.

And judgment is difficult to automate because judgment depends heavily on context.

So what should organizations actually do?

I don't think the answer is to reject AI.

That would be backwards.

The answer is to become much more intentional about what AI should automate and what humans should continue learning.

For example:

1. Protect apprenticeship

Not every inefficient task is useless.

Some tasks are inefficient because they are training.

That distinction matters.

2. Measure correction cost

If AI produces 10x more output but humans spend most of their time correcting it, the organization needs to know.

Output alone is not productivity.

3. Redesign incentives

If employees are rewarded simply for producing more, AI will naturally create more low-value output.

Quality and judgment need to remain part of the incentive system.

4. Keep the talent pipeline alive

Entry-level hiring isn't only about today's workload.

It is also an investment in tomorrow's expertise.

5. Treat AI strategy and talent strategy as the same problem

The CTO can decide what technology to deploy.

The CHRO can decide how talent is developed.

But AI changes both simultaneously.

Separating those decisions creates blind spots.

The uncomfortable conclusion

I don't think AI is inherently destructive to human expertise.

The more interesting possibility is this:

Organizations can accidentally destroy expertise through the way they deploy AI.

They can automate the training ground.

They can reward volume over judgment.

They can remove entry-level opportunities.

They can turn experienced employees into AI-output reviewers.

And then, years later, wonder why they cannot find enough people who actually understand the system.

That is why I don't see AI talent risk as merely an HR problem.

It is an organizational architecture problem.

The technology is advancing extremely quickly.

The question is whether our systems for developing human capability can advance with it.

Because if we optimize only for what AI can do today, we may accidentally weaken what humans need to be capable of tomorrow.
